## Claims

### Header fields (subtitle, card, highlights, statistics)

**H1.** Subtitle: "A company whose own researcher built a digital camera in 1975" — see claims 2 and 3 (MIL).

**H2.** Subtitle: "but whose profits came from film, paper, chemicals and processing" — see claims 5, 6 and 8 (K96).

**H3.** Subtitle: "the very things that camera made unnecessary" — see claims 10 and 11 (K03, K05).

**H4.** Card subtitle: "It built the digital camera, but film paid the bills." — see claims 2 (MIL) and 5–8 (K96). "Built the digital camera" rests on Kodak's own description ("Kodak invented the world's first digital camera"); the body hedges it as "what the company calls the world's first".

**H5.** Highlight 1: "A Kodak researcher built a digital camera prototype in 1975, about the size of a toaster." — claims 2 and 3 (MIL).

**H6.** Highlight 2: "Film, paper, chemicals and processing could earn again each time someone took a photo." — claims 5–7 (K96, K03).

**H7.** Highlight 3: "Digital revenue overtook traditional revenue in 2005, while gross margin fell to 25.6%." — claims 16 and 17 (K05).

**H8.** Statistic "1975 — Digital Camera Prototype" — claim 2 (MIL).

**H9.** Statistic "~48% — Gross Margin, Fiscal 1996" — claim 8 (K98/K96; arithmetic shown there).

**H10.** Statistic "25.6% — Gross Margin, Fiscal 2005" (trend down) — claim 17 (K05).

### Section 1 — A Photograph of Film

**1.** "The object above is a photographic print of film: one 35mm roll in its small metal canister, the leader curling out across a darkroom counter, more canisters and loose strips around it."
- Not a factual claim about the company: it describes the article's cover image as specified in the assignment brief.

**2.** "In 1975 a Kodak researcher built what the company calls the world's first digital camera"
- Source: MIL, entry for 1975 (block "1973-1975") and entry for 2010.
- Excerpt (1975): "1975 - Kodak invented the world's first digital camera."
- Excerpt (2010): "Retired Kodak researcher Steve Sasson received the National Medal of Technology and Innovation for invention of the digital camera in 1975."
- Note: the person is deliberately not named in the article (role only, "a Kodak researcher"). "What the company calls" attributes the "world's first" description to Kodak.

**3.** "a toaster-sized prototype that captured black-and-white images of 10,000 pixels"
- Source: MIL, entry for 1975.
- Excerpt: "The prototype was the size of a toaster and captured black-and-white images at a resolution of 10,000 pixels (.01 megapixels)."

**4.** "Kodak did not miss the future. It invented it, then had to work out how to live with it."
- Interpretive sentence resting on claim 2 (MIL: "Kodak invented the world's first digital camera"). No additional fact asserted.

**4a.** "The film in the picture and the paper it is printed on are, between them, a fair portrait of how Eastman Kodak made its money."
- Source: K96, Item 1 Business (page 2) and Item 7 MD&A, "1996 compared with 1995" (page 9).
- Excerpt (Item 1): "For amateur photography, Kodak supplies films, photographic papers, processing services, photographic chemicals, cameras and projectors."
- Excerpt (MD&A): "Strong color film and paper volumes, photofinishing increases in Qualex and sales of Advantix products led the gains."

### Section 2 — A Meter on Every Picture

**5.** "Kodak's annual report for fiscal 1996 spelled out the model. Amateur photography needed, at minimum, a camera, film and photofinishing, and photofinishing needed chemicals and paper for prints."
- Source: K96, Item 1 Business, Consumer Imaging Segment (page 2).
- Excerpt: "traditional amateur photography requires, at a minimum, a camera, film and photofinishing. Photofinishing requires equipment and supplies, including chemicals and paper for prints."

**6.** "Kodak supplied all of it."
- Source: K96, Item 1 Business, Consumer Imaging Segment (page 2).
- Excerpt: "For amateur photography, Kodak supplies films, photographic papers, processing services, photographic chemicals, cameras and projectors."

**7.** "The camera opened the door; the pictures paid the rent. Each roll bought, developed and printed was another chance to sell film, paper, chemicals or processing."
- How-the-business-works statement. Sources: K96 (claims 5, 6) plus K96 MD&A and K03 Item 1.
- K96, Item 7 MD&A, "1996 compared with 1995" (page 9): "Consumer Imaging segment sales for the year were up 12% … Strong color film and paper volumes, photofinishing increases in Qualex and sales of Advantix products led the gains."
- K03, Item 1 Business, Photography Segment (page 4): "Other digitization options have been created to stimulate more pictures in the use and printing of these images, adding to the consumption of film and paper."
- K03, Item 1 Business, Marketing and Competition (page 5): "The Company also has photofinishing laboratories in many parts of the world and supplies photographic papers and chemicals to other entities that provide photofinishing services."
- "Pictures paid the rent" is a metaphor for the above; "chance to sell" is deliberately non-absolute (competitors existed: K96 Item 1, "Competition in traditional imaging markets is strong throughout the world.").

**8.** "In fiscal 1996 Kodak reported about $16.0 billion of sales and roughly $7.6 billion of gross profit, close to 48 cents of every sales dollar."
- Source A: K98, Consolidated Statement of Earnings (page 30), column 1996.
- Excerpt: "Sales $13,406 $14,538 $15,968 / Cost of goods sold 7,293 7,976 8,327 / Gross profit 6,113 6,562 7,641" (columns 1998, 1997, 1996; in millions).
- Source B (cross-check): K96, Consolidated Statement of Earnings (page 13): "Sales $15,968 $14,980 $13,557" and "Cost of goods sold 8,326 7,962 7,325" (columns 1996, 1995, 1994; in millions).
- Arithmetic: $7,641M / $15,968M = 47.85% → "close to 48 cents of every sales dollar"; statistic "~48%". $15,968M → "about $16.0 billion"; $7,641M → "roughly $7.6 billion".
- Note: K98 MD&A also reports a 1996 gross margin of 49.9% computed after "Removing the impact of the Office Imaging business from both years"; the article uses the unadjusted reported totals.

**9.** Subheading "What the digital camera deleted" — framing, no fact.

**10.** "A digital camera has no roll to replace: the next picture needs no film, no chemicals and no lab."
- How-the-business-works statement. Source: K03, Item 1 Business, Marketing and Competition (page 5); K05, Item 1 Business.
- K03 excerpt: "digital product offerings are substituting for some of the traditional film and output products, primarily in the U.S., Japan and Western Europe, as a large number of consumers actively use digital cameras."
- K05 excerpt: "Film usage continues to decline as consumers continue to migrate from a film-only household, to a dual-use (digital camera and film) household, to a mobile phone use household."

**11.** "Kodak's fiscal 2003 annual report named the damage. Digital substitution had hit mainly its film and paper sales and processing services"
- Source: K03, Item 1 Business, Photography Segment, Marketing and Competition (page 5).
- Excerpt: "While this substitution to date has had an impact primarily on the Company's film and paper sales, and processing services in the U.S., Japan and Western Europe, the Company's strategy is to offset this by providing its own digital products, digitization services and output services."

**12.** "and it expected U.S. consumer film industry volumes to fall 10% to 12% in 2004."
- Source: K03, Item 1 Business, Photography Segment (page 4).
- Excerpt: "For 2004, the Company estimates that consumer film industry volumes will decline in the U.S. by 10% to 12% and worldwide by 7% to 9% primarily due to digital substitution."

**13.** Callout (highlight): "Before asking whether an incumbent can build the new product, ask what the new product deletes. When it deletes the repeat purchase that pays for everything else, the company must replace its income, not just its product line."
- Transferable lesson (opinion), resting on claims 5–8 and 10–12. No new fact.

### Section 3 — Winning the Sales, Losing the Margin

**14.** "It introduced a professional digital camera system in 1991"
- Source: MIL, entry for 1991.
- Excerpt: "The KODAK Professional Digital Camera System (DCS) was introduced, enabling photojournalists to take electronic pictures with a Nikon F-3 camera equipped by Kodak with a 1.3 megapixel sensor."

**15.** "launched EasyShare consumer cameras in 2001"
- Source: MIL, entry for 2001.
- Excerpt: "The KODAK EASYSHARE System, a new line of digital cameras and docking systems that set the standard for ease of use for digital photography, was launched."

**15a.** "and, by its own count, shipped its 100 millionth digital still camera in 2011."
- Source: MIL, entry for 2011.
- Excerpt: "Kodak shipped its 100 millionth digital still camera, placing the company among top manufacturers worldwide over the past 15 years."

**15b.** "Kodak did go digital, and seriously." — summary of claims 14–15a, 15c and 16 (K05 also: "through the 2005 acquisitions of KPG and Creo, Kodak has essentially completed its $3 billion investment and acquisition plan included in the 2003 strategy").

**15c.** "Its 2003 strategy paired disciplined management of the traditional business for cash with faster digital growth."
- Source: K03, Item 1 Business (page 3).
- Excerpt: "There are four key elements of the strategy: - Disciplined management of traditional imaging businesses to maximize cash generation over an extended period of time - Accelerated growth in the Company's existing portfolio of digital imaging products and services"
- Cross-check: K05, Item 1 Business: "In 2003, Kodak announced a comprehensive strategy to be implemented through 2007 to complete its transformation as the leader of the traditional photographic industry to a leadership position in digital imaging markets."

**16.** "By fiscal 2005 digital revenues had overtaken traditional ones."
- Source: K05, Item 1 Business (strategy discussion).
- Excerpt: "For the year ended December 31, 2005, Kodak achieved a level of digital revenues that exceeded traditional revenues."

**17.** "On about $14.3 billion of sales, gross margin slipped to 25.6% from 29.1% a year earlier"
- Source: K05, Item 7 MD&A, 2005 compared with 2004 (Net Sales; Gross Profit).
- Excerpt (sales): "Net worldwide sales were $14,268 million for 2005 as compared with $13,517 million for 2004"
- Excerpt (margin): "The gross profit margin was 25.6% in the current year as compared with 29.1% in the prior year."
- $14,268M → "about $14.3 billion".

**18.** "and the annual report named consumer digital cameras first in explaining the price and mix declines."
- Source: K05, Item 7 MD&A, Gross Profit, 2005 compared with 2004.
- Excerpt: "The 3.5 percentage point decrease was primarily attributable to declines that reduced gross profit margins by approximately 3.8 percentage points due to: (1) price/mix, driven primarily by consumer digital cameras, one-time-use cameras, traditional consumer and digital health products and services"

**19.** "Its fiscal 2003 report had already warned that rapid price declines soon after a model's launch were common."
- Source: K03, Item 1 Business, consumer digital business (page 6).
- Excerpt: "The Company faces competition from other electronics manufacturers in this market, particularly on price and technological advances. Rapid price declines shortly after product introduction in this environment are common, as producers are continually introducing new models with enhanced capabilities"

**20.** Callout (warning): "This is a value trap of strategy: a business too profitable to abandon. The further Kodak's sales shifted toward hardware sold once under steady price pressure, the thinner its margin became."
- First sentence: framing/opinion (no stock or price claim).
- Second sentence rests on the reported trend of digital growth led by consumer cameras alongside a falling company gross margin:
  - K03, Item 7 MD&A (page 30): "The gross profit margin was 32.2% in the current year as compared with 35.9% in the prior year." (2003 vs 2002)
  - K05, Item 7 MD&A: "The gross profit margin was 29.1% in the current year as compared with 32.2% in the prior year." (2004 vs 2003) and "The gross profit margin was 25.6% in the current year as compared with 29.1% in the prior year." (2005 vs 2004)
  - K05, Item 7 MD&A (2004 vs 2003): "D&FIS segment digital product sales were $2,641 million for the current year as compared with $1,802 million for the prior year, representing an increase of $839 million, or 47%, primarily driven by the consumer digital capture SPG."
  - K05, Item 7 MD&A (2005 vs 2004): "D&FIS segment digital product sales were $3,412 million for 2005 as compared with $2,641 million for 2004, representing an increase of $771 million, or 29%, primarily driven by the consumer digital capture SPG"
  - Price pressure on cameras: claims 18 and 19.

### Section 4 — Looking for Another Meter

**26.** "Its fiscal 2025 annual report describes a far smaller manufacturer, with about $1.1 billion of revenue and three business segments:"
- Source: K25, Item 1 Business; Item 7 MD&A, "Net Revenues from Continuing Operations by Reportable Segment".

- $1,069M → "about $1.1 billion". "Far smaller" compares with fiscal 1996 sales of $15,968M (claim 8).

**27.** Bullet: "Print: digital offset printing plates, presses, inks and workflow software"
- Source: K25, Item 1 Business, Print.
- Excerpt: "Print segment products include digital offset plate offerings and computer-to-plate ("CTP") imaging solutions, production press systems, consumables (primarily ink), inkjet components, software and services, and high-quality digital printing solutions using electrically charged toner-based technology."

**28.** Bullet: "Advanced Materials and Chemicals: still, industrial and motion picture film, specialty chemicals and patent licensing"

- Excerpt: "Industrial Film and Chemicals : • Offers professional and consumer still photographic film, as well as industrial film, including films used by the electronics industry to produce printed circuit boards. • Includes related component businesses: Specialty Chemicals; Solvent Recovery; and Polyester Film."

Print customers: "The Print segment serves a variety of commercial industries, including commercial print, direct mail, book publishing, newspapers and magazines and packaging/labels."

**33.** "The 1996 and 2005 margins describe different companies, since Kodak sold and bought whole businesses in between, so the comparison shows direction, not precision."

**34.** "And hindsight flatters the critic: the filings show how hard it was to give up a meter that ran on every picture, not what a film company should have become."
- Caveat/opinion; no new fact.

## Claims considered and dropped (could not support from a primary source, or deliberately excluded)

- **"A young engineer"** (the brief's suggested phrasing): no Kodak page or filing found that calls the 1975 inventor young or an engineer; Kodak's own milestones page says "Retired Kodak researcher". The article uses "a Kodak researcher".
- **The inventor's age, the first image's subject, the 23-second cassette write time, the 1978 patent, and "management buried the camera"**: found only in news/encyclopedia sources, never in a Kodak page or filing. Omitted.
- **"Peak" sales / "best year"**: fiscal 1996's $16.0 billion is stated as a fiscal-year figure only; pre-1993 sales included Eastman Chemical (spun off at year-end 1993), so no "peak" claim is made.
- **Kodak's 2004 U.S. digital camera share of 21.9%** (K05 cites an IDC study): available but third-party data relayed in a filing; dropped for length.
- **Ofoto acquisition (2001), DC40 consumer camera (1995), printer docks, kiosks**: supported (MIL, K03) but dropped for length.
- **What happened to the old common stock at emergence** (cancelled per 8K-0903): deliberately excluded under the no-shareholder-loss rule.
